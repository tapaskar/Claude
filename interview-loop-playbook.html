The Four-Round Playbook

Past-project deep dive → AI system design → business case → culture fit. Each round tests a different muscle, and your two prepared corpora — closed-loop network autonomy and LLM serving economics — cover all four. This is the game plan: what each round is really scoring, your winning move, and the trap to sidestep.

R1

Past-project deep dive

They're testing: ownership, depth, and judgment — not the project itself. Interviewers pick one thread and pull until it snaps or proves solid.

Prepare each project at three zoom levels

  • 30 seconds — the headline: problem, your role, the result with one number.
  • 3 minutes — context → constraints → the 2–3 decisions that mattered → outcome → what you'd change.
  • 15 minutes — every decision with the alternative you rejected and the evidence that settled it.
Winning moveThe number audit. For every figure you plan to cite (GPUs sized, throughput, cost, latency, % improvement), write down how it was measured. "Walk me through that number" is the most common drill — and the sizing math is now reflex for you: weights = params × bytes, KV per token, bandwidth ÷ bytes for decode, Little's law for concurrency, $/1M from GPU-hours.

For the DataHouse GPU-sizing story specifically

  • Lead with the method, not the answer: "I sized from four budgets — weight memory, KV/working memory, bandwidth-bound throughput, and cost per token at target utilization."
  • Have ready: what you assumed (batch, context length, utilization %), what surprised you, and where reality diverged from the model.
  • Expect: "Why that GPU and not X?" "What if traffic doubled?" "What did you over- or under-provision?" — each is a chance to show the capacity-vs-bandwidth-vs-compute-bound diagnosis.
TrapA story with no failure. Prepare one real miss — a wrong assumption, an outage, a sizing error — plus the fix and the systemic change (a check, a review step, a monitor) that came from it. That answer builds more trust than any success story.

Drill questions to rehearse against

"What was YOUR specific contribution vs. the team's?"

Name the pieces you personally owned ("I built the sizing model and ran the load tests; a teammate owned the deployment pipeline"). Naming collaborators honestly raises credibility. Alternate "I" for your decisions and "we" for shared outcomes.

"Why did you choose X? What else did you consider?"

Always answer in the shape: options → criteria → evidence → decision → what would have changed my mind. If you considered nothing else, say what you'd evaluate today — never pretend the alternative analysis happened if it didn't.

"What would you do differently now?"

Give a real technical regret (e.g., "I'd have load-tested with our actual input/output length distribution instead of a synthetic 128-token benchmark — it mispredicted concurrency") plus one process regret (e.g., "I'd have set eval gates before the first model swap").

R2

AI system design: the autonomous network ops assistant

They're testing: structure under ambiguity, safety thinking, and whether you've operated real systems. If the prompt is a generic RAG/agent design, the same skeleton applies — swap the domain nouns.

The 40-minute script

  1. Clarify (5 min). Who uses it (NOC engineers? on-call?), and — the money question — does it advise or act? Read-only triage vs. executing changes are different systems. Also: scale (alarms/day, engineers), latency needs, data available, regulatory/data-residency constraints.
  2. Skeleton (10 min). Draw the three-plane diagram below. Narrate left to right, then the safety envelope.
  3. Deep dive (15 min). Let them pick a plane — retrieval, orchestration, safety, or serving. Have depth ready in each.
  4. Eval & rollout (5 min). Shadow → suggest → gated execute. Nobody else mentions this; you should.
  5. The trace (5 min). Walk one incident end-to-end. Concreteness is what interviewers remember.
The framing sentence to open with"An ops assistant isn't one big brain — it's an agent sitting inside a closed loop: awareness → analysis → decision → execution, with feedback. The LLM lives at the slow, human-facing altitude; sub-second control stays algorithmic. And the design's center of gravity is the safety envelope between the agent and the network."

The seven decisions to narrate (with the "because")

  • The LLM stays out of the fast loop. Sub-second corrections (load balancing, protection switching) stay algorithmic; the LLM operates at the minutes-scale analysis/decision altitude and the human interface. An LLM's latency and failure modes disqualify it from real-time control — saying this out loud is a senior tell.
  • Two retrieval kinds, never one. Topology, inventory, and telemetry are queried live via tools — embedding them would serve stale state. Runbooks, past incidents, and vendor docs are indexed (hybrid BM25 + dense, reranked, answers must cite sources).
  • Actions go through intent APIs, not raw device config. The agent emits a declarative "what" (TMF921-style intent, or a ticket/change request); domain controllers own the "how." Bounded interface, auditable, reversible.
  • Confidence gating + blast-radius tiering. Every proposal carries a confidence score and an impact tier (read-only → draft ticket → single-cell change → wider). High confidence and small blast radius may auto-run later; everything else escalates to a human with the evidence attached.
  • Transactional execution. Dry-run/what-if first where a twin exists; execute; verify the KPIs/KQIs actually recovered within a time budget; auto-rollback on failure. Idempotent, desired-state actions so retries and rollbacks are safe.
  • HITL → HOTL progression, per scenario. Launch human-in-the-loop (approve before act) on everything; graduate individual, well-measured scenarios to human-on-the-loop (act, human can veto). Autonomy is granted per scenario, never as a global badge.
  • Eval-gated rollout. A golden set of past incidents; measure RCA top-3 accuracy, remediation acceptance rate by real engineers, citation faithfulness. Ship shadow → suggest → gated execute, with rollback criteria. Any model/quant/prompt change re-runs the gate.

The serving sidebar — your home turf, volunteer it

Model & hosting: telco operational data rarely leaves the operator's boundary → default to a self-hosted open-weight model in their VPC (a 70B-class dense in FP8 on 2×H100, or an MoE like gpt-oss-120b in MXFP4 on a single 80 GB H100 — memory from total params, speed from ~5B active). Optionally a frontier API fallback for the hardest cases if policy allows.

Serving: vLLM with continuous batching; prefix caching is a genuine win here — every request shares the long system prompt and tool schemas, so TTFT drops sharply. SLO: TTFT under ~1–2 s streaming, ~30–50 ms/token; that's a copilot, not real-time control, which is exactly why the LLM lives at this altitude.

Cost sanity check: at ~$0.2–0.5 per 1M tokens well-batched, an incident consuming ~50k tokens costs a fraction of a cent — against minutes of engineer time. The GPU bill is a rounding error; the eval and integration effort is the real cost. Saying that unprompted is a business-round preview.

The trace — rehearse this until it's fluid

  1. Transport link degrades → alarm fan-out across RAN and Core (hundreds of correlated alarms).
  2. Assistant queries topology + telemetry (tools), correlates the storm to one probable root; retrieves a matching past incident and the runbook section (cited).
  3. Emits: "Probable root: fiber degradation on link X, confidence 0.87. Impact: 3 enterprise slices at SLA risk. Proposed: reroute via Y (blast-radius tier 2). Evidence: …"
  4. Tier 2 → one-click human approval → executes via intent API → verifies KQIs recover in the verification window → posts the incident summary to the ticket.
  5. Counterfactual: had confidence been 0.5, it escalates with the evidence bundle instead of acting — the safe-degradation path is part of the design, not an afterthought.

If the prompt is plain "design a RAG system"

Same skeleton, fewer nouns: clarify corpus/users/freshness → ingestion (chunk by structure, not fixed size; keep metadata/ACLs) → hybrid retrieval + reranker → grounded generation with citations and an "I don't know" path → eval (retrieval recall and answer faithfulness, separately) → freshness and access control → serving/cost. The differentiators are the same two: separate structured-live from unstructured-indexed data, and eval as a first-class component.

Probes they'll throw

"What about hallucinations? This is a network — a wrong action is an outage."

Three layers: grounding (answers must cite retrieved sources or live tool output; ungrounded claims are suppressed), the safety envelope (an LLM never touches the network directly — confidence gate, impact tier, dry-run, human approval, verify + rollback), and eval (faithfulness measured on the golden set, shadow mode before any action rights). The design assumption is that the model will sometimes be wrong — the system is built so a wrong proposal cannot become an outage.

"Why not fine-tune instead of RAG?"

The knowledge is fast-moving and auditable — topology changes daily, runbooks get edited, and answers must cite sources for trust and compliance. RAG + tools handles freshness and attribution; fine-tuning bakes in stale facts and gives no citations. Where fine-tuning (or LoRA) earns its place: output format, domain jargon, tool-calling reliability — a small adapter on the base model, cheap to serve alongside it.

"How do you know it's working?"

Offline: golden incident set — RCA top-1/top-3 accuracy, remediation acceptance rate, citation faithfulness. Online: MTTR and triage-time deltas measured against a holdout of un-assisted incidents, engineer thumbs-up rate, and the one that matters for trust: zero incorrect automated actions, verified by the transactional execute-verify path. Report per-scenario, and gate every change on re-running the offline suite.

"How does this scale to 100k alarms a day?"

The LLM should never see 100k alarms. Classic correlation/dedup (topology-aware, algorithmic) compresses the storm first; the agent reasons over the compressed summary plus targeted tool queries. Serving-side: continuous batching, prefix caching for the shared prompt, and replicas sized with Little's law (concurrent incidents × tokens per incident). The expensive resource is engineer attention, not GPU time — the design optimizes what reaches a human.

R3

Business-case discussion

They're testing: can you turn technology into money, honestly — with unit economics, sensitivity, and a plan that survives contact with a CFO.

The five-beat structure

  1. Quantify the baseline. Incidents/month, mean triage and MTTR minutes, loaded engineer cost, SLA-credit exposure. No baseline, no case.
  2. Lay out the options. Do nothing / buy a SaaS-API solution / build on open weights / hybrid. Name the honest strengths of each — including "do nothing."
  3. Unit economics. Value per incident vs. cost per incident. Your cost formula: $/1M tokens = GPU-$/hr ÷ (tok/s × 3600 ÷ 1e6); then the truthful add-on — the GPUs are cheap, the team (platform, eval, on-call) is the cost line that decides build-vs-buy.
  4. Sensitivity. "What would have to be true": the case survives if triage improves ≥ X% and acceptance rate ≥ Y%; it dies if engineers ignore the suggestions. State the kill criteria yourself.
  5. Phased plan. 1: shadow + golden-set eval (no user impact). 2: suggest-only on 2–3 scoped scenarios, measure MTTR delta on a holdout. 3: gated execution for the top scenario. Expand only on evidence.

A worked mini-case (adapt the numbers to theirs)

NOC handles 10,000 incidents/month, mean triage 25 min. Assistant cuts triage 30% → 10,000 × 7.5 min ≈ 1,250 engineer-hours/month ≈ ~7 FTE-equivalents ≈ $0.8–1M/year of capacity (at ~$120k loaded) — before counting faster MTTR reducing SLA credits.

Cost side: 2×H100 nodes reserved ≈ tens of $k/year — negligible. The real line: 1.5–2 platform/ML engineers + eval infra ≈ $400–600k/year. Net: positive if the 30% holds; the pilot exists to prove that number, and the kill criterion is written down before we start: if suggestion-acceptance is under ~40% after one quarter, we stop and re-scope.

Winning moveVolunteer the honesty items before they ask: token cost is a rounding error, people cost dominates; autonomy is per-scenario and starts at suggest-only; and API-per-token beats self-hosting at low or spiky volume — the crossover is a utilization bet, and data-residency may decide it regardless of price.
TrapPromising "full autonomy" or hockey-stick ROI. The credible pitch is boring: scoped scenarios, measured deltas, expansion gated on evidence. Boring wins the room.
R4

Culture fit

They're testing: self-awareness, collaboration, and how you behave when you're wrong. This round is lost by winging it, not by lacking stories.

Build a six-story bank (STAR-L: Situation, Task, Action, Result, Learning)

ThemeThe question shapes it answersWhat the ending must contain
Disagreement"Tell me about a technical conflict"You argued with data, and either changed their mind or changed yours — both are wins
Failure"A time you were wrong / something broke"Your mistake named plainly, the fix, and the systemic change
Ambiguity"Vague goal — what did you do?"You imposed structure: clarified the objective, cut scope, shipped a v1
Influence"Convincing without authority"A prototype, a measurement, or a doc did the convincing — not volume
Lifting others"Mentoring / making the team better"Someone else's win, enabled by you, that outlived your involvement
Hard feedback"Toughest feedback you've received"Real feedback, initial sting admitted, concrete behavior change since

The AI-responsibility question — you have a real answer

When they ask "how do you think about AI safety in ops automation"Don't give platitudes — give the framework you actually hold: "Autonomy is earned per scenario, never granted globally. Every automated action carries a confidence score and a blast-radius tier; below the gate it escalates to a human with evidence instead of acting; execution is transactional — verify the effect, roll back on failure; and humans move from in-the-loop to on-the-loop only as measured trust accumulates. The system is designed assuming the model will sometimes be wrong." That's a values answer with engineering underneath it.

Questions to ask them (pick three)

  • "How do model or prompt changes get validated before production — is there an eval culture, or is that something to build?"
  • "Who decides when an automation graduates from suggest-only to acting? What's the worst automated action that's happened?"
  • "What would 'great' look like for this role at six months — and what's the failure mode you've seen in this seat?"
  • "What's the honest state of the data — can the assistant actually query topology and telemetry today, or is that the first year of work?"
✓

Night-before checklist

Ninety minutes, in this order

  1. Say each project's 30-second version out loud, twice.
  2. Redraw the architecture diagram from memory on paper — planes, two retrieval kinds, safety envelope, feedback arrow.
  3. Recite the five formulas: weights = params × bytes · KV = 2·layers·kv_heads·head_dim·bytes · decode ≈ BW ÷ bytes · concurrency = rate × latency · $/1M = $/hr ÷ (tok/s × 3600 ÷ 1e6).
  4. Rehearse the incident trace once, including the low-confidence counterfactual.
  5. Skim your six stories — headline and learning line only.
  6. Write the three questions you'll ask them. Sleep.
