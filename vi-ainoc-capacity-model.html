01

What the constraint changes

Not the measured capacity — same model, same token shapes, same tok/s per GPU. What changes is allocation and the HA posture.

One pool, two service classes

The five SoW agents put tokens through a language model in only two places: the GenAI RFO Agent (an agentic evidence loop over SOP/MOP, OEM and 3GPP material — 85% of all token demand) and the Action & Interface Agent (ticket text, shift handover, operator Q&A).

These have opposite service shapes, which is why the earlier design gave each its own hardware. At this scale that was wrong: the interactive workload is only 0.24 GPU of load. It never justified a dedicated GPU. Both classes run the same model, so one vLLM deployment serves both, with priority scheduling protecting the interactive class.

Correlation, anomaly detection and prediction stay classical ML plus deterministic rules — CPU, not GPU, and they carry the volume.

Why the fractions add

A workload needing demand ÷ capacity of a GPU can share that GPU with another. Under a work-conserving scheduler the requirements are additive, which is exactly what lets two pools collapse into one:

A separate sweep put both traffic shapes through one deployment and measured 5,433 tok/s — 1.373 GPUs of work, 3.8% worse than the additive estimate. Two independent methods agreeing that closely is the strongest evidence in this model, and every headroom figure below is derated by that 3.8%.

What neither method settles is how chunked prefill and priority scheduling interact once the classes really share a GPU. That is the one item to verify on hardware before committing.

What was measured vs. modelled

Measured by GuideLLM: request rate, end-to-end latency, TTFT, ITL, concurrency and their full percentile distributions — real load generation, real scheduling, real statistics, real mixed traffic.

Supplied by the latency model: this container has no GPU, so the backend is guidellm mock-server with TTFT and ITL pinned per operating point to published gpt-oss-120b + vLLM figures for a single H100 80GB. The saturation point reproduces the reported ~6,100 tok/s.

One consequence to be honest about: the mock applies one latency profile per run, so the shared-pool sweep proves the throughput blend but not the interaction between long prefills and short interactive requests. Priority scheduling has to be validated on hardware.

The gate that decides everything

RFO request latency p95 ≤ 30 s. The RFO must reach the incident well inside the ticket window; 30 s leaves budget for retrieval, orchestration and the HPSM write.

Interactive TTFT p95 ≤ 1,500 ms and ITL p95 ≤ 25 ms — at least 40 tok/s, faster than anyone reads.

In the shared pool the RFO gate governs the aggregate; the interactive gate is held by giving that class scheduling priority.

KV cache is not the constraint

A 120B-class MoE at MXFP4 leaves 10–14 GB free on an 80 GB H100. At ~72 KB of KV per token that is 21–30 concurrent 6.4k-token requests per GPU. Peak needs 8–16. Memory has room; the binding constraint is compute, which is what the sweep measures.

04

Does it fit in three?

Yes — with genuine margin, and with N+1 intact. Here is the whole calculation.

Utilisation of measured knee capacity at peak hour, by failure state.
StateGPUs servingLoad required % of capacityVerdict
All three healthy, average load3 0.57 GPU19.1% Idle most of the day
All three healthy, peak hour3 1.37 GPU45.8% Meets SLO with margin
One GPU failed, peak hour2 1.37 GPU68.6% Meets SLO — this is the N+1 case
Two GPUs failed, peak hour1 1.37 GPU137.3% Breaches SLO

Why three is the right number, not two

Two GPUs would also meet the SLO at peak — 69% of capacity. But two GPUs means no N+1: a single GPU failure at peak hour puts you at 137% and the RFO queue grows without bound.

Three is the smallest fleet where a GPU can fail during the busiest hour of the day and the platform still meets its commitments. That is the entire justification for the third card — not throughput.

Four buys the ability to absorb a doubling of RFO demand, or an agent loop that grows from three turns to six. It is the right next purchase, not a launch requirement.

Graceful degradation is what makes this safe

The two classes fail differently, and that asymmetry is why a small fleet works. Interactive traffic is 0.24 GPU — even one surviving GPU serves it comfortably. RFO generation is batch-shaped: during a failure it can stretch from a 9-second p95 to a minute without anyone being harmed, because nothing is waiting on a cursor.

So the degradation ladder is explicit: shed RFO latency first, protect interactive always, and never let a GPU failure touch the deterministic alarm-to-incident path — which does not use a GPU at all.

The one that is a real loss: single site

Everything else on that list was over-provisioning. The second site was not — dropping it means a data-centre outage takes the AI-NOC offline entirely. The mitigation is real but partial: during phased rollout the NOC's existing manual process still exists and stays staffed, so a DC failure degrades the operation back to today's baseline rather than stopping it. That mitigation expires the moment headcount comes out.

So the honest sequencing is: run single-site through Phase 1 and the shadow period, and fund the second site before the manpower reduction lands, not after. Three GPUs is a launch configuration, not a steady-state one.

A correction to the earlier model

The 14-GPU design specified two active sites but reported a single-site CPU platform. Run properly, two active sites would have needed 976 vCPU and 88 TB, not 488 and 44. That was understated.

Going single-site makes the 488 vCPU / 43.9 TB figures correct as published. It also means the true saving from this descope is larger than the GPU line alone suggests.

The context budget is now load-bearing

In the 14-GPU design a per-agent context budget was good practice. At three GPUs it is the control: 6,000 tokens works, 9,600 is the ceiling, and the 24k-context scenario needs five GPUs on its own.

Make it a hard cap enforced in the agent code — chunk count, chunk size and rerank depth all fixed — with an alert when p95 prompt size crosses 8,000. A retrieval change that quietly doubles context is now a capacity incident, not a tuning choice.

What to instrument from day one

  • p95 prompt tokens per agent — the leading indicator for every scenario above
  • Incidents/day and RFO share — the two volume dials, both visible in the existing outcome dashboards
  • Agent turns per RFO — a loop that starts averaging four turns is a fourth GPU in about a quarter
  • Peak-hour GPU utilisation against the 66% N+1 line — not against 100%

The token table per agent is already in the storage plan (1.5 TB of platform metrics). This is what it is for.

07

The platform underneath

Unchanged by the GPU decision, and still the larger machine. It produced the most counter-intuitive result in the model.

11 of 13 components are floor-bound

At Vi's event rates almost nothing is sized by throughput. 194 alarms/second at the peak hour is a rounding error for Kafka or Flink. What sets the size is the operational floor — the smallest replica anyone deploys in production: JVM heap, compaction, rebalance headroom, index build, GC — times the three-replica quorum every stateful component needs.

The practical consequence: this platform does not get cheaper if the alarm volume halves. It gets cheaper only by removing components. That is the scope conversation, and it is why the 285 GB/day KPI feed — not the alarms — is the only stream that behaves like a firehose.

08

Verify before you commit

Three GPUs has less margin than fourteen, so the two open questions matter more. Both are a day's work on one rented card.

# 1. serve the candidate model
vllm serve openai/gpt-oss-120b --port 8000 --max-model-len 32768 \
  --enable-chunked-prefill --scheduling-policy priority

# 2. THE TEST THAT MATTERS: mixed traffic, both classes at once
#    Does interactive TTFT hold when long RFO prefills share the GPU?
guidellm run \
  --backend kind=openai_http,target=http://localhost:8000,model=openai/gpt-oss-120b \
  --data "kind=synthetic_text,prompt_tokens=6000,prompt_tokens_stdev=1200,output_tokens=400" \
  --data "kind=synthetic_text,prompt_tokens=2700,prompt_tokens_stdev=900,output_tokens=466" \
  --profile kind=concurrent,streams=8 \
  --override 'profile.streams' 1,4,8,16,32 \
  --constraint kind=max_duration,seconds=120 \
  --output kind=html,path=shared.html --output kind=json,path=shared.json

# 3. re-derive the fleet
python vi_demand.py && python vi_capacity.py && python vi_3gpu.py

The two things to prove

  1. Interactive TTFT under mixed load. If priority scheduling does not hold TTFT p95 under 1.5 s while RFO prefills run, the classes need separating again — and that is a fourth GPU. This is the only assumption in the design that cannot be checked by arithmetic.
  2. Real capacity per GPU. Prefix caching should help materially: a large share of every RFO prompt is the same SOP/MOP preamble, so measured capacity will likely come in above this model. That widens the 1.57× headroom.

Run the sweep before the procurement

The whole fleet number rests on one measured quantity — tok/s per GPU at the SLO — and it moves by a factor of three across plausible model, quantisation and context-length choices. Two hours on one rented H100, before anyone signs, is the highest-leverage two hours in the programme.

Then re-run on every model change. Capacity is not a property of the hardware; it is a property of the hardware, the model, the quantisation and the token shape together.

09

Defending this in the room

Four things worth being able to say without notes.

  1. "Three, because of N+1 — not throughput." Peak-hour demand across both workloads measures 1.37 GPUs. Two would meet the SLO; three is the smallest fleet where a GPU can fail during the busiest hour and the platform still meets its commitments.
  2. "I collapsed the pools because the interactive load was 0.24 of a GPU." Two pools was the right architecture and the wrong sizing. Same model, one deployment, priority scheduling — and the one thing arithmetic cannot settle, interactive TTFT under mixed prefill, is a day's test on a rented card.
  3. "The context budget is now load-bearing." 6,000 tokens works, 9,600 is the ceiling, 24,000 needs five GPUs. A retrieval change that doubles context is a capacity incident, so the budget is a hard cap in code with an alert at 8,000.
  4. "Single site is the one real loss, and it has an expiry date." Everything else we dropped was over-provisioning. The second site is fundable later — but before headcount comes out, because after that the manual fallback no longer exists.
