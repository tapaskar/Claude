01

The shape of the problem

Sizing an LLM platform is one division: demand ÷ capacity. Almost everyone gets it wrong by guessing the denominator. GuideLLM exists to measure it.

What is actually AI here

The SoW names five agents. Only two of them put tokens through a language model:

  • GenAI RFO Agent — an agentic evidence loop doing retrieval over approved SOP/MOP, OEM and 3GPP material, producing a grounded RCA/RFO with citations. This is 85% of all token demand.
  • Action & Interface Agent — ticket enrichment text, shift handover, work-order content, operator Q&A.

Correlation, anomaly detection and prediction are classical ML plus deterministic rules. They need CPU, not GPU — and they carry the volume.

Why two pools, not one

These two agents have opposite service shapes, so they get separate serving pools with separate SLOs:

  • Pool A RFO generation is throughput-shaped. Nobody is watching a cursor blink; the RFO must be attached to the incident inside the ticket window. Gate on end-to-end latency, run near the throughput knee.
  • Pool B Operator chat and narratives are latency-shaped. A human is waiting. Gate on time-to-first-token and a readable streaming rate.

Sizing them as one pool forces the interactive SLO onto the batch workload and over-buys GPUs.

The compression trap

This funnel gives 99.5% alarm-to-incident compression against the SoW's ≥90% target. That is only honest if the baseline is measured at our tap point. If Vi filters or cleans alarms upstream before we receive them, the denominator is already compressed and the target becomes unfair in both directions. This is the first thing to settle in the data workshop — and the reason the design retains suppression evidence (IDs and reason, not payloads) so the ratio is auditable.

What was measured vs. modelled

Measured by GuideLLM: request rate, end-to-end latency, TTFT, ITL, concurrency and their full percentile distributions — real load generation, real scheduling, real statistics.

Supplied by the latency model: this container has no GPU, so the backend is guidellm mock-server with TTFT and ITL pinned per operating point to published gpt-oss-120b + vLLM figures for a single H100 80GB. The saturation point reproduces the reported ~6,100 tok/s.

On real hardware you change one flag — point --backend at the vLLM endpoint — and every number on this page re-derives.

The gate that decides everything

Pool A request latency p95 ≤ 30 s. An RFO must reach the incident well inside the ticket window; 30 s leaves budget for retrieval, orchestration and the HPSM write.

Pool B TTFT p95 ≤ 1,500 ms and ITL p95 ≤ 25 ms, i.e. at least 40 tok/s — faster than anyone reads.

Headroom, deliberately

Capacity is derated to 70% of the SLO-passing point. Utilisation and p95 are not linear: as ρ approaches 1, queueing delay grows like ρ/(1−ρ). Running at the knee means the first traffic burst breaks the SLO. The 30% is not waste, it is the difference between a p95 you can promise and one you can only hope for.

Why 14 and not 2

Peak-hour demand needs 1.9 GPUs. Everything above that number is bought for reasons that have nothing to do with token volume:

  • N+1 within each site, so a GPU failure is not an outage
  • Two sites, because a national NOC platform cannot have a single-DC dependency
  • Two non-prod for the eval harness and model bake-offs — without these you cannot safely change models
  • Two shared for anomaly/prediction training, used off-peak

Average utilisation of the serving fleet lands near 20%. That is not over-provisioning; it is what peak-plus-HA looks like. Anyone who prices this at average load will be wrong twice a day.

Model and memory fit

A ~120B-class MoE at MXFP4 lands near 62 GB, so it fits one 80 GB H100 with room for KV cache — no tensor parallelism, no NVLink dependency, and a failure domain of exactly one GPU. That is the single most useful property for an HA-shaped fleet.

If quality review forces a dense 70B at FP8 (~70 GB), it needs 2×H100 with TP for KV headroom, the failure domain doubles and the GPU count roughly doubles with it. Model selection is a capacity decision, not only a quality one — re-run the same sweep before committing.

05

The platform underneath

This is where the real machine is. And it produced the most counter-intuitive result in the model.

11 of 13 components are floor-bound

At Vi's event rates, almost nothing is sized by throughput. 194 alarms/second at the peak hour is a rounding error for Kafka or Flink. What sets the size is the operational floor — the smallest replica anyone actually deploys in production: JVM heap, compaction, rebalance headroom, index build, GC. Add the three-replica quorum every stateful component needs and you arrive at 488 vCPU without the workload ever being the constraint.

The practical consequence: this platform does not get cheaper if the alarm volume halves. It gets cheaper only by removing components. That is the conversation to have about scope, and it is the reason the 285 GB/day KPI feed — not the alarms — is the only stream that behaves like a firehose.

Node count

At 96 vCPU / 768 GB per dual-socket node the fleet is RAM-bound, not CPU-bound (7 nodes for memory, 6 for cores). Graph and vector stores must stay resident. Buy memory.

06

Where the answer breaks

Capacity is measured; demand is assumed. So every argument about fleet size is really an argument about one of these dials.

Sensitivity. The baseline is 268.5M tokens/day and 10 production GPUs.
If this is wrong…tok/day × baseEffect on fleet ServingProd GPU

Context length is the dominant lever

Moving the RFO prompt from 6k to 24k tokens multiplies total demand 3.5× on its own — more than tripling the incident count does. Retrieval discipline (how many chunks, how big, reranked or not) is therefore a capacity control, and it belongs in the LLD as a hard budget per agent, not a tuning knob someone adjusts in production.

The worst-case row is not a forecast. It is what happens if every dial is left ungoverned at once, and it is why the context budget is a design artifact.

Build vs buy, honestly

10 H100 fully loaded at roughly $3.20/GPU-hour is about $280k/year. The same 98B tokens through a hosted open-weight endpoint at $0.60/1M is about $59k/year. On-prem breaks even near 4.8× today's volume.

So the fleet is not justified on unit cost, and pretending otherwise is the fastest way to lose a CFO. It is justified because the SoW requires approved private or on-prem endpoints — alarm and topology data does not leave. The right framing is data residency and HA, with the token cost being incidental.

07

Reproducing this on real hardware

The point of using GuideLLM rather than a spreadsheet is that the sizing becomes a command anyone can re-run the day the GPUs land.

# 1. serve the candidate model
vllm serve openai/gpt-oss-120b --port 8000 --max-model-len 32768

# 2. sweep it at the Vi RFO token shape, gated on the real SLO
guidellm run \
  --backend kind=openai_http,target=http://localhost:8000,model=openai/gpt-oss-120b \
  --data "kind=synthetic_text,prompt_tokens=6000,prompt_tokens_stdev=1200,\
output_tokens=400,output_tokens_stdev=100" \
  --profile kind=concurrent,streams=8 \
  --override 'profile.streams' 1,2,4,8,16,32 \
  --constraint kind=max_duration,seconds=120 \
  --output kind=html,path=pool-a.html --output kind=json,path=pool-a.json

# 3. same again for the interactive pool
guidellm run ... --data "kind=synthetic_text,prompt_tokens=2000,output_tokens=400" ...

# 4. re-derive the fleet
python vi_demand.py && python vi_capacity.py && python vi_sensitivity.py

What changes with a real backend

  • vLLM reports true usage.prompt_tokens, so GuideLLM's own token counters become authoritative and throughput no longer has to be derived from req/s.
  • Prefix caching starts working. A large share of the RFO prompt is the same SOP/MOP preamble every time — expect measured capacity to come in above this model, not below.
  • Chunked prefill changes the interaction between the two pools if you co-locate them. Size them separately first, then test co-location.

Run the sweep before the procurement, not after

The entire fleet number rests on one measured quantity: tok/s per GPU at the SLO. It moves by a factor of three across plausible model, quantisation and context-length choices. A two-hour sweep on a single rented H100 — before anyone signs for ten — is the highest-leverage two hours in the whole programme.

Then re-run it on every model change. Capacity is not a property of the hardware; it is a property of the hardware, the model, the quantisation and the token shape together.

08

Defending this in the room

Four things worth being able to say without notes.

  1. "I measured the denominator." Most sizing answers guess tokens per second per GPU. GuideLLM is the vLLM project's SLO-aware harness; I swept eleven operating points at our real token shape and took the throughput at the last point that met the SLO — goodput, not peak throughput.
  2. "The LLM tier is the small part." Peak demand needs about two GPUs of serving. The fleet is 14 because of HA, DR, eval and training. The platform is 488 vCPU and 44 TB, and 11 of its 13 components are sized by their operational floor rather than by Vi's event rate.
  3. "Context length is the capacity control." Prompt size moves total demand more than incident count does — 6k to 24k tokens is 3.5×. That is why a per-agent context budget belongs in the LLD as a hard constraint.
  4. "On-prem does not pay for itself here, and that is fine." Break-even is near five times this volume. We build private because the data cannot leave, not because it is cheaper — and saying so is what makes the rest of the business case credible.
