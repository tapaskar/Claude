LLM Serving — the friendly version

How LLMs are actually run, in plain English.

Serving a model is really just two jobs: reading the question, then writing the answer one word at a time. Almost every interview question is about making that second job faster and cheaper. Each idea below starts with a plain-words note (the analogy), then the real thing, then the one mistake to avoid.

Take it slowly, top to bottom. You don't need the numbers memorized — you need the pictures in your head.

1

The 3 ideas everything rests on

Get these three and the rest is just details hanging off them.

IDEA 1

There are two phases: reading, then writing

When you send a prompt, the model first reads the whole thing at once (fast) — this is called prefill. Then it writes the reply one word at a time (slower) — this is called decode. The wait before the first word appears is prefill; the speed the words stream out is decode.

IDEA 2

Writing is slow because of memory, not math

To write each single word, the GPU has to read the entire model out of its memory. The math is quick; the slow part is hauling all those numbers across. So the thing that limits writing speed is memory speed (bandwidth), not how many calculations the chip can do.

IDEA 3

The "notepad" (KV cache) is what really fills up the GPU

As the model works, it keeps a notepad of everything said so far so it doesn't re-read the whole conversation each time. This notepad is the KV cache. It grows with longer conversations and with more users at once — and it usually runs out of room before the model itself does.

2

The core concepts

Same pattern each time: the plain-words note first, then what's really going on.

Concept

Prefill vs. decode

💬 In plain words

Prefill is skimming the whole question in one glance. Decode is writing the answer word by word — and re-reading your entire textbook before every word.

They stress the computer differently. Prefill does a lot of math all at once, so it's limited by raw compute power. Decode does tiny amounts of math but constantly re-reads the model, so it's limited by memory speed. That's why the two need different fixes.

⚠ Watch out:Don't say "make the chip do more math" to speed up writing. Writing is waiting on memory, not math.

Concept

Memory-bandwidth-bound (the big one)

💬 In plain words

It's not that the chef cooks slowly. It's that they run to the pantry and carry every ingredient back for each single bite. The trip is the bottleneck.

Because the GPU re-reads the whole model for every word, faster writing comes from moving fewer bytes (a smaller model — see quantization) or a GPU with faster memory — not from a chip with more math power.

⚠ Watch out:A GPU with huge "TFLOPS" (math power) but the same memory speed barely helps writing. Look at memory bandwidth (TB/s) first.

Say this: "Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so I'd optimize bandwidth and batching before compute."

Concept

Batching (and why vLLM exists)

💬 In plain words

A bus, not a taxi. One trip across town (one read of the model) can carry many passengers (many users' words) at once.

Since reading the model is the expensive part, you serve many users on the same read. That's batching, and it's the single biggest way to get more out of a GPU. vLLM is the popular free software that does this well — it lets users hop on and off the "bus" continuously instead of waiting for it to fill up (continuous batching).

⚠ Watch out:Bigger batches serve more people but make each person wait a little longer. It's a trade-off, not a free win.

Concept

The KV cache (the notepad)

💬 In plain words

A running notepad of the conversation. Instead of re-reading everything said so far, the model jots down notes and glances at them.

The notepad grows with every word and every extra user. Long chats and many users make it balloon — and it, not the model's size, is usually what fills the GPU's memory. So "how many people can I serve at once?" is really "how much notepad room is left?"

⚠ Watch out:"The model fits in the GPU" isn't enough. Ask: how much room is left for the notepad? That's what caps how many users you serve.

Concept

Quantization (shrinking the model)

💬 In plain words

Rounding $19.99 to $20. You store each number with fewer digits — the model gets much smaller, and mostly just as good.

Models are made of billions of numbers. Storing them with fewer bits (16-bit → 8-bit → 4-bit) roughly halves, then quarters the size. Because writing is limited by how much you haul from memory, a smaller model writes faster — and it may now fit on one GPU instead of two.

⚠ Watch out:Shrinking too far (4-bit) can quietly hurt quality on hard tasks like math and code — even if the model "looks" fine. Always test real tasks, not just a single score.

Say this: "Quantization mainly speeds up writing and saves memory; it doesn't magically make everything faster."

Concept

Picking a GPU: "does it fit" + "how fast"

💬 In plain words

Two questions, like renting a truck: Is the cargo bay big enough (memory), and how fast can it drive (bandwidth)?

Rough rule for "does it fit": a model with 70 billion numbers needs about 140 GB at full size — that's two big GPUs. Shrink it (quantize) to ~70 GB and it fits one; to ~35 GB and it fits one comfortably with notepad room. For "how fast," compare memory bandwidth, not the math-power number.

⚠ Watch out:The newer H200 isn't a "faster brain" than the H100 — it just has more memory and faster memory. Great for writing and for fitting more, not for raw math.

Concept

Splitting a model across GPUs

💬 In plain words

Two people carrying one big load. Split the model over two GPUs and each hauls half — so each word is written faster, not just "it now fits."

When a model is too big for one GPU, you spread it across several (tensor parallelism). A nice bonus: because each GPU now reads only its half from memory, writing gets faster too — not merely possible. This only works if the GPUs are joined by a very fast link (NVLink), so it's done inside one machine.

⚠ Watch out:Splitting a model across separate machines over a normal network is slow — the GPUs spend all their time talking. Keep the split inside one box.
3

Making it fast & affordable

The business side — speed promises and money.

Concept

Speed for one vs. total served (and "goodput")

💬 In plain words

A highway. Add more cars and the road carries more people total — but everyone drives a bit slower. Pack it too tight and it jams.

Two different things: how fast one person gets their answer (latency) vs. how many people you serve total (throughput). Bigger batches raise the total but slow each person. The number that actually matters is goodput: how many people you serve while still hitting your speed promise.

⚠ Watch out:A huge "tokens per second" number is meaningless if every user is now too slow. Always tie throughput to a speed promise.

Two words to know: time-to-first-word (the initial wait) and time-per-word (how fast it then streams).

Concept

Cost per answer

💬 In plain words

You rent the GPU by the hour whether it's busy or idle. Cost per answer is just: rental price ÷ how much work you squeezed out that hour.

So the price of the GPU matters far less than keeping it busy. A well-batched server can be 10× cheaper per answer than a poorly-used one on the exact same hardware. When people quote a cost per million tokens, the hidden question is always "at what usage level?"

⚠ Watch out:Never estimate cost from one user at a time — that overstates it wildly. Real cost assumes the GPU is packed with users (batched).

The formula, plainly: cost per million words = (GPU price per hour) ÷ (words per second × 3,600) × 1,000,000. Rounded: a busy $16/hr server doing 3,000 words/sec is about $1.50 per million words.

Concept

Run your own vs. pay per use (open vs. API)

💬 In plain words

Buying a car vs. taking taxis. Owning is cheaper if you drive a lot and steadily; taxis win if your trips are rare or unpredictable.

Run your own (a free "open-weight" model like Llama, Gemma, or gpt-oss on your GPUs): cheaper if you have steady, heavy traffic — plus you keep your data private and control everything. Pay per use (an API like Claude, GPT, Gemini): no setup, best quality, and cheaper when traffic is light or spiky — you don't pay for idle GPUs.

⚠ Watch out:"Open models are free" — the weights are; running them isn't (GPUs, engineers, on-call, backups). For a small team, the API often wins until traffic is large and steady.
4

The traps (what people get wrong)

If you remember nothing else, remember not to say these.

Quick corrections

⚠"Writing is limited by the chip's math power." No — it's limited by memory speed. This is the #1 thing interviewers listen for.
⚠"The model fits, so we're fine." The notepad (KV cache) usually fills up first. Check the leftover room.
⚠"Quantizing makes everything faster." It mainly speeds writing and saves memory; it barely helps the reading phase.
⚠"Look at the big TFLOPS number to pick a GPU." For writing-heavy work, look at memory bandwidth first.
⚠"Cost per answer from one user." Always assume the GPU is packed with users; otherwise you're off by 10×.
⚠"A 4-bit model is basically 0.5 bytes per number." Close, but it's a touch more (~0.53) once you count the little "scale" values — and shrinking weights doesn't shrink the notepad.

If you only remember 5 things

  1. Two phases: reading the prompt (prefill), then writing word-by-word (decode).
  2. Writing is slow because of memory speed, not math — so speed it up with a smaller model, faster memory, or batching.
  3. The notepad (KV cache) fills the GPU and caps how many users you serve.
  4. Batching = a bus. It's how you make serving cheap; the cost is a little extra wait per person.
  5. Cost is about keeping the GPU busy, not the sticker price. Own vs. API is "car vs. taxi."
